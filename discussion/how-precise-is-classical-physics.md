# How precise is classical physics? (Penrose's examples)

## The Question

Penrose mentions the following descriptions to explain how classical physics is precise:
- In a metre's range, errors in treating the geometry as Euclidean amount to less than the diameter of a hydrogen atom.
- As Galileo and Newton's theory is applied to the motions of planets and moons, the observed accuracy of these theories is phenomenal (better than one part in ten million).
- Maxwell's theory is accurately valid over an extraordinary range (from the tiny scale of atoms to that of galaxies).

Are these true?

## Short Answer

Yes — these are essentially accurate, and they're classic examples Penrose uses (in *The Road to Reality* and *The Emperor's New Mind*) to argue that classical physics, despite being "superseded," is astonishingly precise within its domain.

---

## 1. Euclidean geometry at the metre scale

**True, and actually conservative.** Spacetime curvature near Earth is tiny — the curvature radius induced by Earth's gravity works out to roughly the Earth–Sun distance (~10¹¹ m). Over a 1-metre baseline, the deviation from flat (Euclidean) geometry is on the order of 10⁻²³ m — vastly smaller than a hydrogen atom (~10⁻¹⁰ m). So the claim understates just how good the approximation really is.

## 2. Newton/Galileo for planetary/lunar motion — better than 1 part in 10⁷

**Broadly true** as a statement about the general predictive power of Newtonian mechanics (positions, periods, orbital mechanics) — historically it let astronomers predict eclipses, transits, and even discover Neptune from orbital perturbations.

The one well-known exception is **Mercury's perihelion precession** (~43 arcsec/century unexplained by Newton, a much larger discrepancy, ~1 part in 100), which is precisely the anomaly General Relativity resolved. Penrose's point is usually framed as "even where Newton is *wrong*, it's wrong by only a tiny amount" — so the number is right as a general accuracy bound, with that famous exception being the whole reason we know Newton isn't the final word.

## 3. Maxwell's equations from atomic to galactic scale

**True.** Classical electromagnetism holds without modification across an enormous range — from atomic/molecular electric fields to plasma and magnetic-field behavior in galaxies (tens of orders of magnitude in scale). It only needs to be supplemented (not replaced) by quantum electrodynamics at the smallest scales. This "huge range of validity" is a commonly cited hallmark of Maxwell's theory in physics writing.

---

## Conclusion

All three are legitimate, verifiable examples of classical theories' precision — Penrose uses them to set up his larger argument that a theory can be extraordinarily accurate and still eventually be superseded (setting up his discussion of where quantum mechanics/gravity take over).
